Lami's Theorem

Understand the equilibrium of three concurrent forces.

1. Introduction

Lami's Theorem is an important principle of engineering mechanics used to solve problems involving three forces acting at a common point.

When three coplanar, concurrent and non-collinear forces keep a particle in equilibrium, the relationship between their magnitudes and the angles between them can be expressed using Lami's Theorem.

2. Statement of Lami's Theorem

If a particle is in equilibrium under the action of three coplanar, concurrent and non-collinear forces, each force is proportional to the sine of the angle opposite to it.

3. Conditions for Application

  • Exactly three forces must act on the particle.
  • All three forces must lie in the same plane.
  • Their lines of action must pass through one common point.
  • The forces must be in equilibrium.
  • The forces must not all act along the same straight line.

4. Mathematical Formulation

Let three forces F₁, F₂ and F₃ act at a common point. Let α, β and γ be the angles opposite to F₁, F₂ and F₃ respectively in the triangle of forces.

F₁ / sin α = F₂ / sin β = F₃ / sin γ

Where:

  • F₁, F₂, F₃ = Magnitudes of the three forces.
  • α = Angle opposite to F₁.
  • β = Angle opposite to F₂.
  • γ = Angle opposite to F₃.

For the triangle of forces:

α + β + γ = 180°

5. Diagrams – Understanding Lami's Theorem

Lami's Theorem can be understood using two different diagrams. Both represent the same three-force equilibrium system, but in different ways.

5.1 Concurrent Force Diagram

Three forces act at a common point O. The angles between the forces are measured opposite to their respective forces.

Schematic representation of three concurrent forces. The angle α is between F₂ and F₃, β between F₃ and F₁, and γ between F₁ and F₂.

5.2 Triangle of Forces

The same three forces can be represented head-to-tail. For equilibrium, they form a closed triangle.

Schematic vector triangle. Its internal angles are supplementary to the corresponding angles in the concurrent force diagram.

Understanding the Difference

Concurrent Force Diagram:

Shows the actual directions of the three forces acting at a common point.

Triangle of Forces:

Shows the vector addition of the forces. The head-to-tail arrangement forms a closed triangle because the resultant force is zero.

Why does Lami's formula work?

The internal angles of the force triangle are supplementary to the angles between the forces. However, their sine values are equal.

sin(180° - θ) = sin θ

Therefore, applying the sine rule to the force triangle gives Lami's Theorem.

6. Derivation of Lami's Theorem

Consider three forces F₁, F₂ and F₃ acting on a particle in equilibrium.

Since the particle is in equilibrium, the vector sum of all three forces must be zero.

F₁ + F₂ + F₃ = 0

Therefore, the three forces can be represented by a closed triangle when drawn head-to-tail.

Applying the sine rule to this triangle:

F₁ / sin α = F₂ / sin β = F₃ / sin γ

Hence, Lami's Theorem is obtained.

7. Solved Numerical Example

Three forces keep a particle in equilibrium. If F₁ = 100 N, α = 30° and β = 60°, find F₂ and F₃.

Given:

F₁ = 100 N
α = 30°
β = 60°

Step 1: Find the third angle.

α + β + γ = 180°

30° + 60° + γ = 180°

γ = 90°

Step 2: Apply Lami's Theorem.

F₁ / sin α = F₂ / sin β = F₃ / sin γ

Step 3: Calculate F₂.

100 / sin 30° = F₂ / sin 60°

F₂ = 100 × sin 60° / sin 30°

F₂ = 100 × 0.866 / 0.5

F₂ = 173.2 N

Step 4: Calculate F₃.

100 / sin 30° = F₃ / sin 90°

F₃ = 100 × 1 / 0.5

F₃ = 200 N

Final Answer: F₂ = 173.2 N and F₃ = 200 N

8. Engineering Applications

  • Finding tension in cables supporting a suspended body.
  • Analysing forces at pin joints and connections.
  • Solving problems involving suspended weights.
  • Studying equilibrium of rings connected by strings.
  • Analysing simple structural and mechanical systems.
